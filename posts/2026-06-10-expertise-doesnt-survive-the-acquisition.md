Aaron Brethorst wrote a piece last month called [Domain Expertise Has Always Been the Real Moat](https://www.brethorsting.com/blog/2026/05/domain-expertise-has-always-been-the-real-moat/) that I haven't stopped thinking about. His argument: the hard part of software was never writing the code, it was building a working model of the domain in your head first. Agentic AI made the writing cheap. Knowing what *right* looks like is the part that's still scarce, and it's the part that's now worth the most.

I want to extend that argument, because there's a second force at work that makes it even more true, and I see it up close every day in our industry.

## Where the expertise goes

Vertical software has been consolidating for years. A founder who lived the problem builds a tool. It works, because every screen and every default in it encodes something they learned the hard way. It grows. Eventually it gets acquired, then merged with three adjacent products, then run as a portfolio.

Nothing about that is evil. It's just how the market works. But watch what happens to the domain expertise along the way.

The founder leaves after the earn-out. The support people who knew why a particular validation rule existed get consolidated into a shared services team that covers six products. The roadmap stops being driven by "what does a person doing this job actually need" and starts being driven by retention metrics and cross-sell targets. The people making decisions about the product have often never done the work the product exists to support — and increasingly, neither has anyone who reports to them.

The software still runs. The logo is still on the invoice. But the working model of the domain — the thing Aaron correctly identifies as the actual moat — has quietly walked out the door. What's left is a transcription with no one around who can still read the original.

## Meanwhile, a new horizontal AI tool every day

At the same time, the market is flooding with horizontal AI products. Every week there's a new general-purpose agent that promises to handle your back office, your finance ops, your compliance, your whatever. They demo beautifully, because demos are made of plausible outputs.

But plausible is exactly the problem. Aaron's framing applies perfectly here: these tools have no oracle. A horizontal product can generate an answer for any industry precisely because it holds the ground truth for none of them. It can produce something that looks like a correct indirect rate calculation, or a compliant invoice, or a clean reconciliation. Looking like one and being one are different things, and the difference is where businesses live or die.

The incumbents can't save you from this, because they've lost their oracle too. And the horizontal tools never had one.

## The thing the incumbents forgot

Here's the misunderstanding at the center of all of it: the companies that own legacy vertical software believe they're selling software. They're not. The software was always just the container. **The data drives the systems, and the systems drive the businesses.** Every number that flows through these tools is load-bearing — it determines what a company can bill, what it can bid, whether it passes an audit, whether it makes payroll.

If you understand that, you build very differently. You obsess over whether the numbers are right, not whether the feature list is long. You design for the person who has to certify those numbers with their name on them. You treat every edge case not as a support ticket but as a piece of domain knowledge to encode.

If you don't understand that — if you think you're selling seats and modules — you end up shipping software *at* an industry instead of *for* it. And no amount of bolted-on AI fixes that, because AI amplifies whatever understanding you bring to it. Amplified shallow is still shallow.
