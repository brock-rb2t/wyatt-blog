Over the last few months we've started going on site with the firms rolling out Wyatt, sitting in their offices and watching how their teams actually use it. Some firms are taking off and some are stuck, and the difference has surprisingly little to do with the software.

This week I listened to Lenny's conversation with [Josh Woodward](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/why-every-company-now-needs-to-think), who runs Google Labs, the Gemini app, and AI Studio. Lenny's thesis for the episode is that every company now needs to think and operate like a labs team, because what's possible is changing so fast that you have to get good at trying a bunch of stuff before your competition does. That sounds like a Silicon Valley problem. After watching AEC and GovCon firms roll this out, I think it applies to a 60-person engineering firm just as much as it does to Google.

So here's what I'm seeing work, what I'm seeing fail, and where I'd start if I were running one of these firms.

## what's working

**Start with something small and annoying.** The firms getting traction don't open with their biggest problem. They start with the thing that eats 30 minutes every Friday, like a project status summary somebody builds by hand, or a utilization question that normally means waiting on a report. Josh's team actually gives out a golden bandaid award to people who fix paper cuts, the little annoyances that add up in a product. I love that, because inside a firm the paper cuts are where AI earns trust first.

**Keep the teams tiny.** Bezos had the two-pizza rule: if a team couldn't be fed by two pizzas, it was too big. I've heard people float a two-slice rule for the AI era, and Josh basically confirmed it. He said a new Labs project used to be about five to seven people and is now about two to three, maybe four. That matches what I see on site almost exactly. A project manager and an accountant who sit near each other start poking at things together. A principal and a marketing coordinator decide to see if they can turn a proposal around faster. Small groups, close to the actual work.

**Find your frontier people.** Josh said every company needs a set of people whose job is to be at the frontier, the ones with "eight, $200 a month subscriptions on everything." Every firm we visit has one or two of these folks already. They're usually not in IT. They're the project accountant who taught herself Python or the PM who's been using three different AI tools on the side for a year. Find them, give them room, and make what they build visible to everyone else.

**Pair freedom with accountability.** The leaders who do this well are pretty direct about it. Here are the tools, I expect you to use them, and I'm going to look at who is. Then they get out of the way on *how*. Freedom without the expectation turns into nobody logging in. Expectation without the freedom turns into people checking a box.

**Plan in short cycles.** Josh said his team plans on a rolling six months, and their best teams go from an idea to a meaningful milestone in 50 to 100 days. For most firms I'd shrink that further. Pick a 90-day window, set a couple of goals for what should be different at the end of it, and re-plan when you get there. The tools will have changed by then anyway.

## what's not working

**Trying to microwave it.** My favorite line from the episode was Josh saying, "You can't microwave good ideas." He said the best ideas very rarely come from design sprints or a block on the calendar where everyone sits down to come up with the next thing. They show up on weekends, on breaks, when somebody has been hacking on something and can't help themselves. I've watched the microwave approach play out at firms. Leadership decides it's going to "do AI," books a series of workshops, and expects the ideas to come out of the room. They mostly don't, because nobody in that room has used the tools long enough to know what they can do for *them*.

**Going big too fast.** This is the one I see most. A firm picks the gnarliest legacy workflow it has, the one everyone has complained about for a decade, and spins up a 20 or 30 person task force to replace it end to end. Josh called out hiring too many people too fast as a common mistake and told a story on himself. He was on a project years ago that grew to 30 engineers, and he was thrilled about it. They had zero product-market fit, but he said they had a very inspiring vision. You can probably guess how that ended. Headcount on an AI initiative feels like progress, and it's usually a vanity metric.

**Cleaning it up too early.** Once a few people start experimenting, the instinct is to consolidate. Standardize the prompts, pick the official use cases, write the policy, make it neat. Josh said he's learned the hard way that sometimes you have to live in the messiness a little and see what emerges. You still need guardrails around data and security. Inside those guardrails, let it be messy for a while.

**Leaders deciding what works.** "The team usually knows before the leader knows." Josh said that about when to kill an idea, and he told a story about a PM who told him a Gemini feature they were all excited about wasn't good enough to launch. He thanked her for it on a reply-all. In a firm, the people doing the billing and the scheduling and the proposals will know within a week or two whether something is actually saving them time. Ask them, and make it safe for them to say it isn't.

## small fires stack

The best way I can describe what the successful firms do is that they light a bunch of small fires. None of the early wins are earth-shattering on their own. What's interesting is what happens after the first two or three. People start filling in the gaps with their own imagination. If it can do that, can it do this? They find the edges of the tools fast, usually faster than we would have found them for them, and then they start pushing on those edges in directions I wouldn't have guessed. Honestly, a lot of that ends up on our roadmap.

Josh has a version of this he calls the "almost possible" list. His team keeps a running list of things that are just out of reach, and when a new model makes one of them work, they jump on it. Firms can do the same thing at a much smaller scale. Keep a list of the stuff people wish the tools could do. Every few months, check it again. You'll be surprised how many have crossed over.

And here's the part that surprised me. The big, complex legacy workflow that the task force wanted to tackle on day one tends to get solved anyway. It just gets solved later, by the people who've been lighting small fires for a few months and actually understand what's possible. They walk into that problem with a pile of wins behind them and a much better sense of where the tools are strong and where they still need a human.

If I were running a firm and starting on Monday, here's what I'd do:

1. Put the tools in everyone's hands and say plainly that you expect them to be used.
2. Find the two or three people who are already obsessed and give them some time and some cover.
3. Form a few pods of two or three people around small, annoying problems they actually have.
4. Set a 90-day window and resist the urge to standardize anything until it ends.
5. Celebrate the small wins out loud. Get yourself some golden bandaids.

You can't microwave this stuff. But if you give people room and keep lighting small fires, it stacks a lot faster than you'd think.
