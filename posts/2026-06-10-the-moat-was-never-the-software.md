Aaron Brethorst wrote a piece last month called [Domain Expertise Has Always Been the Real Moat](https://www.brethorsting.com/blog/2026/05/domain-expertise-has-always-been-the-real-moat/) that I haven't stopped thinking about. His argument: the hard part of software was never writing the code, it was building a working model of the domain in your head first. Agentic AI made the writing cheap. Knowing what *right* looks like is the part that's still scarce, and it's the part that's now worth the most.

I want to extend that argument, because I think it explains why this is the most exciting moment in decades to be a domain expert who builds.

## Expertise moves

Vertical software consolidates, it always has. A founder who lived a problem builds a tool, it works because every screen and every default encodes something they learned the hard way, it grows, and eventually it becomes part of something bigger. That's a healthy market doing what healthy markets do.

But here's the thing about domain expertise: it doesn't consolidate along with the software. It lives in people. When products merge and teams reshuffle, the deep understanding — why that validation rule exists, what a person doing this job actually needs at 4:55 on the last day of the month — tends to drift away from the product and back out into the world. The software keeps running. The understanding goes wherever the people go.

For a long time that didn't matter much, because the understanding alone couldn't do anything. Knowing exactly what the right tool would look like was worthless if you couldn't spend years and millions building it. The expertise was stranded.

## A new tool every day

The market is filling up with horizontal AI products, and honestly, a lot of them are remarkable. Every week there's a new general-purpose agent that can draft, summarize, reconcile, generate. The raw capability is real and it keeps compounding.

But Aaron's framing applies to every one of them: capability without ground truth is just plausibility. A horizontal tool can produce an answer for any industry precisely because it holds the deep truth of none of them. It can generate something that looks like a correct rate calculation or a clean reconciliation. Whether it *is* one — that judgment has to come from somewhere, and it can only come from someone who has lived in those inputs and outputs for years.

That's not a knock on the tools, it's just the reality of building tools that do hard things in vertical spaces. All of that capability is sitting there waiting for an oracle. Pair it with someone who knows what right looks like, and you get something neither could be alone.

## The data drives everything

Here's what living inside an industry teaches you: the software was never really the product. The software was always just the container. **The data drives the systems, and the systems drive the businesses.** Every number that flows through these tools is load-bearing — it determines what a company can bill, what it can bid, whether it passes an audit, whether it makes payroll.

If you understand that, you build very differently. You obsess over whether the numbers are right, not whether the feature list is long. You design for the person who has to certify those numbers with their name on them. You treat every edge case not as a support ticket but as a piece of domain knowledge to encode.

If you don't understand that — if you think you're selling seats and modules — you end up shipping software *at* an industry instead of *for* it. And no amount of bolted-on AI fixes that, because AI amplifies whatever understanding you bring to it. Amplified shallow is still shallow.

## The moat is ours to build with

So I read Aaron's piece as very good news. The thing that was always scarce — a deep, verified model of a real domain and the people who actually do the work — is still scarce, and it just became the single most valuable input to building software. It can't be rolled up and it can't be prompted into existence. 

Our tagline is "*fast is fine, but accuracy is everything*," and it's not a slogan, it's the thesis. Speed is what AI made cheap, but accuracy is what domain expertise makes possible. In a market where consolidation is draining expertise out of the incumbents and horizontal AI is shipping plausibility at scale, the tools that survive will be the ones built by people who can look at an output and know — instantly, from years of lived experience — whether it's right.
